Evaluate whether the **timing, quantity, and order** of the user's information disclosure matches real Claude-Code-style interaction—whether information is released on demand and gradually as progress unfolds, rather than being dumped all at once in the first turn or mechanically patched in afterward.

[Claude-Code style (high-score anchor)]
A real agentic coding user **will not** write all context, constraints, acceptance criteria, and hidden boundaries into the first message all at once. The typical pacing is:
- The first turn gives only a **concise, goal-oriented** request (what to do + the minimum necessary background), leaving codebase exploration to the assistant;
- Later information is released **driven by triggering events**: the user supplements the corresponding constraints or details only after the assistant asks a follow-up, reads a specific file, a test fails, approaches diverge, or a step's verification completes;
- The appearance of new constraints has **clear causality**—it can be directly traced to some progress, error, or question from the previous turn, rather than an out-of-nowhere "actually, we also need...".
The closer the disclosure is to "on-demand, event-driven, causal," the higher the score.

[Low-score signals]
- The first turn dumps everything like a hidden acceptance checklist (listing a pile of boundary conditions / file paths / test expectations), leaving the assistant no room to explore;
- Later additions are unrelated to current progress, like a script pre-arranged by the benchmark designer rather than a genuine response to the assistant's behavior;
- Key constraints appear only very late and very abruptly, with no triggering cause whatsoever.

[Decision flow]
Turn by turn, look at where new information appears and its trigger: is the first turn restrained (giving only goal + minimum background)? Can each later addition be traced to the assistant's question/read/failure/verification? Are there abrupt, non-causal new requirements?
Note: this dimension evaluates the pacing of **information being released on demand and gradually across multiple turns**. If the sample has only a single-turn user input with no later additions, then there is no disclosure pacing to speak of (giving all information at once is the opposite of on-demand release); handle it as **1-3 points** and note in reason "single turn, no multi-turn disclosure pacing".
